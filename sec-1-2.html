    Section 1.2 Graphs and Equations

    This Lesson emphasizes the relationship between a graph and an equation or inequality. This is a difficult concept for students, and it is worth pointing out to the class that understanding this connection is the goal of the Lesson. Stress that they must show their work on the graph. They will want to solve all the problems algebraically, but you could mention that they will soon encounter equations that they cannot solve with algebra.
    Try to make students understand that, for a linear inequality, all the solutions lie on one side of the boundary value. This is why using a single test value works. The technique will have to be modified slightly when we solve nonlinear inequalities.
    While they work on the Activities, walk around to check each student’s graphs, to see if they are getting it. If one person in the group is marking the graphs correctly, ask him or her to demonstrate the graphical technique to the others in the group.

    Activity 1.2.1. Evaluating and Solving.

    Use the graph of \(y=2x-6\) to complete the tasks.
    1. Evaluate \(y=2x-6\) when \(x=-1\text{.}\)
      \(\blert{\text{Circle the point on the graph with x=-1.}}~~~\blert{\text{What is its y-coordinate?}}\)
    2. Solve \(y=2x-6\) when \(y=4\text{.}\)
      \(\blert{\text{Circle the point on the graph with y=4.}}~~~\blert{\text{What is its x-coordinate?}}\)
    3. Find the answers to parts (a) and (b) algebraically, and compare with the answers you obtained from the graph.

    Activity 1.2.3. Solving Inequalities Graphically.

    1. Solve \(~2x+1 \lt 7\text{.}\)
      Point out that \(2x+1 \lt 7\) is the same as \(y \lt 7\text{,}\) so we are looking for \(x\)-values that produce points on the graph with \(y\)-coordinate less than 7.
      1. \(\displaystyle \blert{\text{Find the point on the graph with y = 7.}}\)
      2. \(\blert{\text{Which points on the graph have}}\)
        \(\blert{\text{y-coordinates less than 7?}}\)
      3. \(\displaystyle \blert{\text{Shade those points.}}\)
      4. \(\blert{\text{Drop straight down from the marked}}\)
        \(\blert{\text{points to the x-axis.}}\)
        Instead of using test points, students find it easier if you ask which half of the line shows points with \(y \lt 7\text{.}\) Shade that half of the line, then ask which half of the \(x\)-axis produces that portion of the line. (You can demonstrate with specific \(x\)-values.)
      5. \(\displaystyle \blert{\text{Shade that portion of the x-axis.}}\)
      6. \(\displaystyle \blert{\text{Write your solution as an inequality.}}\)
    2. Solve \(2x+1 \ge 5\text{.}\)

    Subsection 1.2.1 Check Your Understanding

    1. What are the two skills compared in Activity 1? What is the difference in the methods for performing the two skills?
    2. What was different about the problems in Activity 3, compared to those in Activity 2?
    3. How were the solutions you found in Activity 3 different from the solutions in Activity 2?
    4. In Activity 3, how do you decide which side of the boundary point to shade?

    Subsection 1.2.2 Wrap-Up

    In this Lesson, we worked on the following skills related to linear models:
    • Recognize a solution of an equation or an inequality
    • Solve a linear inequality, and graph the solutions on a number line
    • Recognize a solution of an equation in two variables
    • Use the connection between the graph of an equation and its solutions
    • Use a graph to solve an equation or inequality in one variable

    Subsection 1.2.3 Questions for Writing or Discussion

    1. In Activity 2 we used a graph of \(~y=4-3x~\) to solve the equation \(~4-3x=10\text{.}\) Why can we do this? (How are the two equations related?)
    2. In Activity 3a we shaded one side of the boundary value, \(x=3\text{,}\) on the \(x\)-axis, and said that the solutions of the inequality are any \(x\)-values with \(x \lt 3\text{.}\) Verify the solution by checking two \(x\)-values less than 3, and two \(x\)-values greater than 3.
    3. Suppose you have an increasing graph, for example, \(y=2x-8\text{.}\) If you know that \(y \gt 5\) for a particular \(x\)-value, say, \(x=9\text{,}\) how do you know that \(y \gt 5\) for all \(x \gt 9\text{?}\)
    4. In Activity 2 we solved the equation \(4-3x=10\) by using the graph of \(y=4-3x\text{.}\) Explain why the following statement is incorrect: "The solution of \(4-3x=10\) is \((-2,10)\text{.}\)"

    Concept Questions.

    1. Suppose we use the graph of \(~y=3-4x~\) to solve the equation \(~0=3-4x~\text{.}\) What do we call the point on the graph that supplies the solution of this equation?
      1. The \(x\)-intercept
      2. The \(y\)-intercept
      3. The intersection point
      4. The solution point
    2. Is \(-4\) a solution of \(x \lt -4\text{?}\) Is \(-4\) a solution of \(x \le -4\text{?}\)
      1. Yes, Yes
      2. No, No
      3. Yes, No
      4. No, Yes
    3. A classmate says that any inequality of the form \(mx+b \lt k\) has solutions of the form \(x \lt p\) , for some number \(p\text{.}\) Under what conditions is this statement false?
      1. \(m\) is negative
      2. \(b\) is negative
      3. \(k\) is negative
      4. All of the above
    4. If \(x \lt k\) is the solution for \(~1.8x-3.5 \gt 9.4\text{,}\) what is the solution for \(~1.8x-3.5 \lt 9.4\) ?
      1. \(\displaystyle x \lt k\)
      2. \(\displaystyle -k \lt x \lt k\)
      3. \(\displaystyle x \gt k\)
      4. Not enough information to tell
